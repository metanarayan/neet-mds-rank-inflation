Statistical evidence brief · exam-integrity litigation

A rank list that inflates at the top and nowhere else

The published NEET-MDS results for 2024, 2025 and 2026 show a large, escalating excess of high scorers that grows disproportionately at the very top, is decoupled from the middle of the distribution, and is not accounted for by the modest growth in candidates.

Source NBEMS published rank lists (2024 · 2025 · 2026) Cohorts 27,993 / 29,219 / 30,386 candidates Basis Raw score /960 vs all-India rank Scope Aggregate only — no candidate identified

Ordinary causes of higher scores — an easier paper, a stronger cohort, a bigger field — lift the whole distribution. What the NEET-MDS lists show instead is a top that pulls away while the middle stays put: the number of candidates scoring 600+ doubled from 2024 to 2025 while the median fell by two marks, then nearly doubled again in 2026.

This pattern is consistent with a compromise of the results and is difficult to reconcile with the innocent explanations an examining body would normally offer. It does not, on its own, identify the mechanism or the beneficiaries — which is precisely why the disclosure sought in the petition is necessary to test it.

FINDING 01

The same performance now buys a far worse rank

For any given rank, the raw score needed to reach it climbed every year — by roughly 60 to 85 marks between 2024 and 2026.

2024 2025 2026
Read it this way: a raw score of 600/960 ranked about 470 in 2024, about 1,050 in 2025, and about 1,770 in 2026 — the identical mark sliding nearly four rank-thousands in two years. The 2025 curve rides above 2024 only at the top ranks and rejoins it lower down; the 2026 curve sits above both across the entire list.
FINDING 02

An excess of high scorers the baseline can’t produce

Holding 2024’s distribution shape fixed and scaling only for the larger field, the number of 600+ scorers expected in 2026 is about 517. The list contains 1,795.

Candidates scoring ≥ 600
Candidates scoring ≥ 700

Against its own 2024 baseline, 2026 carries roughly +1,280 excess candidates at 600+ (3.5×) and +180 excess at 700+ (9.3×). The field itself grew about 4% a year. Excess of this size, concentrated where it is, is not a scaling effect — the higher the score band, the larger the multiplier.

FINDING 03

The tell: the top exploded while the middle stood still

Indexing both measures to 2024 = 100 separates the two most cleanly. In 2025 the count of 600+ scorers more than doubled while the median score did not move.

Candidates scoring ≥ 600 (index) Median score (index)
Why this matters: an easier paper or a genuinely stronger cohort raises the median along with the top. Here, between 2024 and 2025, the top tail grew +102% while the median moved −0.8%. A distribution can only deform this way if something lifts high scorers specifically, not the field as a whole — and this appeared a full year before the 2026 result under challenge.
FINDING 04

The whole curve is deforming at its right edge

Plotted as density — the share of each year’s cohort at every score — the body of the distribution barely shifts while the upper tail thickens year on year.

2024 2025 2026 shaded region: 600+
The top-heaviness index confirms it: the ratio of candidates at 650+ to those in the 500–599 band runs 0.08 → 0.15 → 0.25. The extreme upper tail is growing about half again as fast as the band just beneath it — the signature of a top that is being lifted, not a field that is improving.
CONTEMPORANEOUS RECEPTION

A harder, longer paper — that somehow scored higher

Independent same-day reviews of the 2 May 2026 paper reached a consensus opposite to what the results imply. The direction of the contradiction is the point.

Independent review of the paper
  • Rated moderate to difficult — analytical, conceptual and application-based.
  • Widely called lengthy and time-consuming; many could not finish in time.
  • New question forms: image-based, clinical-scenario, and time-bound sections.
  • A deliberate shift away from rote recall toward applied problem-solving.
What the published scores did
  • Qualifying cutoff jumped 261 → 308 — the sharpest single-year rise in recent cycles.
  • Topper score rose 779 → 802 out of 960.
  • Candidates at 700+ multiplied roughly tenfold over two years.
  • The whole distribution shifted up, not down.
A more demanding paper should depress scores, not lift them. The “easier paper” explanation — the one an examining body reaches for first — is contradicted at source by the reviewers themselves. That leaves two possibilities the raw marks cannot separate without disclosure: a subset with prior access to the paper, or an undisclosed post-hoc adjustment of scores.

The official cutoffs independently corroborate the analysis in Findings 01–04: the General/EWS qualifying marks of 261 (2025) and 308 (2026) fall exactly on the medians computed from the rank lists. The inflation is visible in NBEMS’s own published cutoffs, not only in this reconstruction.

Environmental context

2026 was a year of confirmed medical-entrance compromise in India. The undergraduate NEET-UG 2026 paper was cancelled on 12 May 2026 after investigators found overlaps between a pre-circulated guess paper and the actual paper; the CBI subsequently found the 2025 undergraduate paper had been compromised by the same network, and made arrests. That exam is conducted by a different body (NTA, not NBEMS) and proves nothing about NEET-MDS directly — but it establishes that leak concerns in this cycle are documented fact, not conjecture. Separately, after the MDS result, aspirants publicly debated a possible leak, and a transparency demand arose from candidates whose recall-based scores did not match their official marks — the same discrepancy at issue in this petition.

INTERPRETATION

Why the ordinary explanations do not fit

Each routine cause of higher scores predicts a footprint the data does not show.

Claim“The 2026 paper was easier.”
An easier paper lifts the whole distribution, median included. But independent same-day reviews judged the 2026 paper harder and lengthier, not easier (see Contemporaneous reception) — so the premise fails at source. And in 2024→2025 the median even fell two marks while 600+ scorers doubled.
Claim“More candidates sat the exam.”
The field grew about 4% per year. Over the same span the 700+ count grew roughly tenfold. Scaling explains a few percent, not a multiple.
Claim“The cohort was stronger.”
A stronger cohort raises typical performance — the median and the mode — not only the ceiling. A tail that balloons above an unmoved median is not a cohort effect.
Claim“It is normal year-to-year variation.”
The effect is monotone across two consecutive years and grows with score band. Noise does not compound in one direction and concentrate at the extreme.
CANDID LIMITS

What this evidence does not establish

Stated plainly, so the brief survives scrutiny rather than inviting it.

It does not fingerprint a discrete group.

The upper tail is smoothly thickened, not split into a detached second cluster. The data shows the results were inflated; it does not, by itself, isolate a specific set of beneficiaries.

It treats the published score as comparable across years.

Every figure takes “score out of 960” at face value. If grace, moderation or normalisation changed between years, the score itself may not mean the same thing each year — an undisclosed methodology that the petition asks to have revealed.

A statistical anomaly is not proof of a leak.

The pattern is consistent with a compromise; it is also consistent with an undisclosed top-skewed scoring or moderation change. Both require the same remedy: disclosure sufficient to tell them apart.

2024 is the reference, not a certified-clean baseline.

2024 is simply the earliest list available. The tail was already growing by 2025; whether 2024 itself was affected cannot be judged without an earlier year.

THE REMEDY

What disclosure would resolve

The anomaly is testable. Each item below converts it from an inference about shape into a checkable fact about cause.

  1. Candidate response sheets, so the awarded score can be reconciled against the recorded answers.
  2. The master question paper and the official final answer key.
  3. The number and identifiers of deleted, modified, ambiguous or neutralised questions, and the reasons — benchmarked against the 2024 notice, which disclosed the count.
  4. Any grace, bonus, moderation or normalisation methodology applied to the raw scores.
  5. The scoring, percentile and rank methodology in full.
  6. Preservation and production of electronic records — audit trails and server logs — for independent verification.

With these, the excess mass documented above can be traced to a cause: an answer-key or grace decision that lifted the top, a normalisation choice, or a compromise. Without them, the pattern stands unexplained — and an unexplained, escalating, top-concentrated inflation of a professional-entrance rank list is itself the reason disclosure should follow.

Method. Raw scores and all-India ranks were extracted from the three NBEMS published NEET-MDS rank lists (2024: 27,993 records; 2025: 29,219; 2026: 30,386), complete and contiguous by rank. “Expected” counts in Finding 02 apply the 2024 threshold rate to each later year’s cohort size. The density in Finding 04 is each year’s share of candidates per 20-mark band. Index values in Finding 03 are relative to 2024 = 100.

Scope. This is an aggregate statistical analysis of publicly published results. No individual candidate is named, identified or characterised. Nothing here asserts wrongdoing by any candidate; the findings concern the distribution of results and the transparency of the process that produced them.

Note. The separately published 2025 “All-India 50% Quota” list (ranks only, ~16,000 records) is a different document and is not used here; all 2025 figures are from the full published result carrying scores.
